How to Calculate Over/Under Billing on a Construction WIP Schedule

The four numbers a contractor needs for every open job, in the order you calculate them.

Disclosure: Galaxy Forge sells a $49 spreadsheet that implements this method (see the end of the page). This article is complete on its own, and free alternatives exist.

A work-in-progress (WIP) schedule tells you, job by job, how much of the contract you have really earned compared with how much you have billed. Banks, sureties and buyers of a contracting business read it that way, and so should the owner. This is the cost-to-cost method, the most common one for small contractors; your CPA decides which method applies to your business.

The calculation, in order

  1. Revised contract = original contract + approved (signed) change orders.
  2. Revised total cost = cost to date + your honest estimate of the cost still to come.
  3. Percent complete = cost to date / revised total cost (never above 100%).
  4. Earned revenue = percent complete x revised contract.
  5. Over/(under) billing = billed to date - earned revenue. Positive means you have billed ahead of the work.

Worked example

A roofing job has a contract of $38,500. Costs to date are $36,000 and the estimate to complete is $2,500, so the revised total cost is $38,500 and the job is 93.5% complete. Earned revenue is $36,000. The contractor has already billed $38,500, so the job is over-billed by $2,500: the customer has paid for work that is still owed.

A bathroom remodel has a contract of $18,500, costs to date of $14,200 and $6,800 still to come. The revised total cost is $21,000, so the job will finish at a loss of $2,500 (-13.5%). Accounting practice is to recognise an expected loss as soon as it is known; show this line to your CPA.

Mistakes that make the schedule wrong

  • Counting unsigned change orders as contract value inflates earned revenue.
  • Leaving the estimate to complete unchanged for months hides margin fade until the job ends.
  • Treating a positive net over-billing as profit. It is a liability to finish the work.
  • Entering costs that do not agree with the job-cost report.

Where the numbers come from

Cost to date comes from the job-cost report in your accounting system plus invoices received but not yet entered. The estimate to complete should come from the person running the job: remaining labour hours priced at the loaded rate, materials still to buy and subcontract work still to be invoiced.
