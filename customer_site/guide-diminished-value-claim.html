Diminished value claim: what to upload, and what to ask for

Your car was repaired after an accident that was not your fault. The repairs are covered. But the damage record now attached to the vehicle costs you money when you sell or trade it, and your insurer will not raise that unless you ask. This page is the free version of that conversation.

First, check whether you have a claim at all

Diminished value is not owed everywhere, and it is rarely owed against your own policy.

  • Third-party claim. You claim against the carrier of the driver who was at fault. This is the normal route.
  • First-party claim. Georgia is the only US state with a clear legal right to claim diminished value against your own insurer. Elsewhere this generally does not exist.
  • Cosmetic-only repairs on an older vehicle often support little or nothing. Structural and safety-component damage supports more.

Checking eligibility first costs nothing and should come before you spend anything else.

What to upload when the insurer asks for "supporting documents"

This is where most claims quietly stall. The adjuster is usually not permitted to explain what counts, so the claimant sends whatever is nearby and the claim dies at a lowball number. In rough order of how much each item moves the number:

  1. The itemised repair invoice, with parts and labour split out. A single total line reads as cosmetic work. Carriers discount cosmetic and pay more for structural, safety and ADAS/calibration work. Highlight the split and any sensor or calibration line.
  2. Comparable-sales evidence for your exact vehicle. Same year, trim, mileage and options: one clean-condition valuation and one with the accident reported. This is the document that carries a claim.
  3. Dealer or trade-in letters. Two letters stating what a dealer would actually pay less for the damaged vehicle turn an abstract percentage into money.
  4. Damage detail separating cosmetic from structural. Which panels were replaced, whether structural or safety components were involved, and the repair quality. Overstating this is the fastest way to lose credibility on the whole claim.
  5. A written figure with your reasoning attached. Never a bare number.

The 17c formula, and what most people get wrong about it

Almost every carrier quotes the same approach: roughly 10% of the physical damage cost, adjusted for vehicle age and mileage. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners notes this grew out of a single Georgia Supreme Court case and has no legal basis outside Georgia. It is used everywhere anyway, because it produces a small, tidy number.

The NAIC also flags a specific flaw: mileage is already priced into standard retail values, so the mileage modifier can penalise the same factor twice. One formula applied to one vehicle is a starting point for a conversation, not a conclusion.

Use it to sanity-check a low offer. Do not present it as your evidence.

The one sentence that most often moves the number

Ask, in writing: "Please show me the calculation behind that figure."

Insurers are often not required to produce the working and many will decline. But the request itself frequently moves the offer, and a refusal to explain is itself useful information about where the number came from.

Two things to settle before you sign anything

Read the release. A release signed after repairs and after the diminished-value payment can surrender claims you did not know you had. It is routinely the most expensive signature in the file.

Confirm your state's position on first-party diminished value before investing effort in a claim that may not exist where you live.
