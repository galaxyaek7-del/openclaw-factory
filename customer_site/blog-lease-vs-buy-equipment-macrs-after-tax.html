Lease vs Buy Equipment: Compare After Tax, Not by Monthly Payment

Why the monthly payment is not the comparison, and what to compute instead.

Disclosure: Galaxy Forge sells a $39 spreadsheet that implements this method (see the end of the page). This article is complete on its own, and free alternatives exist.

The monthly payment is the wrong comparison. What matters is what each choice costs after tax over the same period, in today's money. This is a calculation method, not tax advice; depreciation rules, Section 179 and bonus limits and lease classification change and depend on your situation, so confirm the treatment with your accountant.

What to put on the buying side

  • The down payment now, and the loan payments each year split into interest and principal.
  • Depreciation under MACRS. For the 5-year class with the half-year convention the percentages are 20.00%, 32.00%, 19.20%, 11.52%, 11.52% and 5.76% (check IRS Publication 946).
  • The tax saved by interest and depreciation: (interest + depreciation) x your marginal tax rate.
  • What you net at the end: resale value, minus tax on the gain over your remaining tax basis, minus the loan still owed.

What to put on the leasing side

Any amount due at signing and the lease payments, which are generally deductible, so each payment costs (1 - tax rate) of its face value. Confirm that your lease is a true operating lease.

Worked example

A $85,000 machine bought with 20% down on an 8% five-year loan has a payment of $1,378.79 a month. Leasing it costs $1,650 a month plus $3,300 at signing. At a 26% tax rate, 5-year MACRS, a $30,000 resale value and a 7% discount rate, buying costs $47,280 after tax and leasing costs $63,376: buying is cheaper by $16,097, 18.9% of the price.

On these assumptions the gap is large enough to matter, but it depends on them: test a lower resale value and a different tax rate before relying on it. The resale value is usually the largest uncertainty: change it and watch the answer move.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Comparing monthly payments only.
  • Ignoring tax on the sale (depreciation recapture).
  • Assuming you can use every deduction when the business has little profit.
