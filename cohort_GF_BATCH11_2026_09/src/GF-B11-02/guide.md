This guide helps direct-to-consumer, e-commerce, fashion, and cosmetics brands that market environmental claims to consumers in the European Union to inventory every claim, link each claim to evidence, score risk under the Empowering Consumers rules applicable from 27 September 2026, remediate high-risk claims, and document review decisions. Dated enforcement landscape, September 2026. The rules applied from 27 September 2026, but transposition is a national patchwork: the Commission opened infringement procedures against 20 member states for late transposition, so enforcement pace differs by market. Belgium transposed with civil and criminal enforcement available from day one (fines up to 10 percent of turnover in serious cases) alongside a six-month administrative grace period to 27 March 2027. Germany applies greenwashing rules to business-to-business practices as well, unlike the consumer-only baseline elsewhere. Brand and company names, plus imagery and colour schemes, can themselves count as environmental claims. Old stock may benefit from phased enforcement only where businesses face genuine transitional difficulties and are taking reasonable compliance steps. Background in plain language. The Empowering Consumers Directive amends unfair commercial practices law to address vague and misleading environmental claims. Its core idea is simple: consumers should see specific, substantiated, and verifiable claims, not broad impressions of sustainability. To deliver that, the directive prohibits certain practices outright, meaning they are unfair in all circumstances without a case-by-case assessment of consumer impact, and it strengthens the general prohibition on misleading actions and omissions for the remaining claims. Member states transpose the directive into national law, and enforcement sits with national authorities and courts. Because transposition details, guidance, and related instruments such as the proposed green-claims substantiation framework continue to develop, always confirm the current applicable national rules with official sources and counsel.

What is banned in the per se sense. Four groups matter most for daily marketing work. First, generic environmental claims without recognized excellent environmental performance or clear specification in immediate proximity, such as eco-friendly, green, sustainable, or climate friendly stated alone. Second, sustainability labels that are not based on certification schemes with transparent criteria and independent verification, or that were not established by public authorities, including many self-created badges and icons. Third, claims that present a legal requirement as a distinctive feature, or that present the environmental benefit of one component as a benefit of the whole product or business. Fourth, carbon-neutral, climate-neutral, or equivalent product claims based on offsetting greenhouse gas emissions outside the value chain. Each group is assessed on the exact wording, placement, imagery, and overall impression seen by the consumer.

What remains allowed when done correctly. Specific, substantiated claims can still be used. A claim that states the exact attribute, the exact scope, the measurement basis, and the time period, placed close to the claim, linked to accessible supporting information, and consistent with the full product life cycle, follows the direction the law favors. Examples of the pattern, each labeled illustrative, include stating recycled content with percentage, scope, standard, and verification; stating reduced packaging weight with baseline year and measurement scope; or stating repairability with the scoring method and model coverage. Certification marks from credible independent schemes can support communication when their criteria genuinely cover the claimed attribute and when use follows the scheme rules. The workbook helps you test each claim against this pattern rather than relying on impression.

Method overview in five phases. Phase 1 Inventory takes one to two weeks and produces a complete Claim Inventory across all channels. Phase 2 Evidence Linking takes two to four weeks and produces an Evidence File Log where every claim has either a linked exhibit or an explicit gap. Phase 3 Risk Scoring takes one week and produces a scored list with high-risk items flagged for action. Phase 4 Remediation takes two to six weeks and produces revised copy, new substantiation, or removals with before-and-after records. Phase 5 Review and Maintenance produces signed Review Worksheets, a publication checklist, and a quarterly monitoring routine. Marketing, product, legal, and e-commerce functions all participate, because claims live in copy, artwork, product data, and media buys simultaneously.

Workflow 1 claim inventory. Step 1 define the capture scope: home page, category pages, product detail pages, cart and checkout, packaging and inserts, paid search and social copy, organic social posts and reels, influencer briefs and gifted-post requirements, marketplace listings, press releases, and customer-service macros. Step 2 copy the exact wording, note the channel, capture the URL or artwork reference and the date observed, and record the owner. Step 3 flag visual cues such as leaves, globes, green color blocks, and badge-like icons, because imagery can create a claim even when words are careful. Step 4 include claims in all languages you publish. Step 5 freeze the inventory with a version date. Illustrative example, labeled illustrative: a fashion brand captures forty-two statements, finding that three product pages, one checkout banner, and two influencer briefs repeat the same generic phrase with different punctuation.

Workflow 2 evidence file log. For each inventoried claim, record the attribute claimed, the scope such as product, component, packaging, or company, the substantiation type such as test report, life-cycle study, certificate, metered data, or supplier declaration, the exhibit reference and date, the issuer and independence status, the coverage match between what was tested and what is claimed, and the expiry or review date. Mark each link as direct, partial, or missing. A life-cycle study for one fabric does not directly support a whole-garment claim. A 2021 energy bill does not directly support a current carbon statement. Supplier declarations need a verification plan, not just a file. Illustrative example, labeled illustrative: a cosmetics brand links a recycled-plastic claim to a supplier certificate covering only caps, marking the bottle-body scope as missing and triggering a reword or new evidence task.

Workflow 3 risk scorer. Score each claim on five factors with one to three points each. Factor 1 generic wording: specific wording scores one, qualified wording scores two, standalone generic wording scores three. Factor 2 substantiation: current direct independent evidence scores one, dated or partial evidence scores two, no evidence scores three. Factor 3 label basis: public-authority or credible certified scheme use scores one, third-party scheme with unclear coverage scores two, own badge without independent scheme scores three. Factor 4 scope honesty: whole matches tested scope scores one, ambiguous scope scores two, part presented as whole scores three. Factor 5 offset reliance: no offset-based neutrality claim scores one, footprint reduction with separate offset disclosure scores two, offset-based neutral claim scores three. Sum the factors. Totals of twelve or above require action before further publication. Calibrate thresholds with counsel.

Workflow 4 remediation tracker. For each claim scoring twelve or above, or flagged by counsel regardless of score, choose retain with new evidence, reword to specific language, relocate context closer to the claim, add accessible supporting information, replace an own badge with compliant text or a qualifying scheme, or remove the claim from all instances. Record the decision, the new wording, the evidence added, the owner, the due date, and the verification date when the live channel was re-checked. Track every instance separately, because fixing a product page while leaving the same phrase in paid search still leaves exposure. Illustrative example, labeled illustrative: a brand replaces a standalone climate-neutral badge with a factual statement about measured reductions, scope, baseline year, and a separate note about any offset purchases kept outside the product claim.

Workflow 5 review worksheet. Close each claim with a one-page style record: claim text, channel, score, evidence references, applicable legal basis considered, decision of retain, reword, or remove, new wording where relevant, reviewer functions, counsel verification date, publication date, and next review date. Keep before-and-after screenshots or artwork exports with dates. File the worksheet with the evidence exhibits so a future reviewer can reconstruct the decision in minutes. Review repeat claims quarterly, seasonal campaigns before launch, and influencer content before posting. Illustrative example, labeled illustrative: a home-care brand records that a refill claim was reworded from a generic green phrase to a specific packaging-weight statement with baseline year and verification reference.

Copy patterns that reduce risk. Favor nouns and numbers over adjectives. State the attribute, the number, the scope, the method or standard, and the date. Place the specification immediately next to the claim, not three clicks away. Keep imagery consistent with words: do not pair a narrow recycled-cap fact with a forest image that suggests broader benefit. Avoid comparisons without a stated baseline, such as cleaner or greener without versus-what and measured-how. Avoid legal-requirement claims such as noting compliance with a mandatory substance restriction as if it were a brand advantage. Keep translations equivalent across languages, because a careful English page paired with a broad translated page still leaves the broad version exposed.

Handling labels, badges, and icons. List every mark that looks like an endorsement, including leaves, checkmarks in circles, green stars, and terms such as certified, verified, or approved. For each mark, record the scheme owner, the public standard or criteria document, the certification scope and certificate number, the audit or verification date, and the usage rules. Retire own badges that lack an independent scheme or public-authority basis. Where a credible scheme genuinely covers the attribute, use the scheme mark exactly as licensed and keep the certificate current. Where no scheme fits, replace the badge with plain specific text plus accessible supporting information. Illustrative example, labeled illustrative: a footwear brand retires two own icons and keeps one independent materials certificate, rewording surrounding copy to match the certificate scope exactly.

Handling carbon and climate language with care. Product-level neutral or net-zero claims based on offsets outside the value chain belong in the remove-or-fundamentally-rewrite group under the per se prohibition. Separate three things that marketing often merges: measured footprint reductions inside the value chain, high-quality information about offsets or contributions outside the value chain, and any suggestion that the product itself has no climate impact. Only the first can support a product attribute claim, and only when specific and substantiated. If the business purchases credits, describe that purchase as a separate contribution with project, standard, vintage, and retirement detail, away from product-attribute language, and only after counsel review. Never imply that buying credits erases the product footprint.

Influencers, affiliates, and marketplaces. Claims made on your behalf count. Review influencer briefs, gifting notes, affiliate copy blocks, and marketplace attribute fields with the same inventory discipline as your own site. Prohibit generic phrases and own badges in briefs, supply approved specific sentences, require pre-publication review of posts, and keep dated copies of what was published. For marketplaces, check auto-generated badges and filter attributes that may attach environmental labels to your listings without your input. Correct errors through the platform process and keep the correspondence. Illustrative example, labeled illustrative: a cosmetics brand finds that an affiliate template repeats an old generic phrase, replaces the template, asks partners to update live posts by a fixed date, and logs completion per partner.

Packaging, inserts, and retail touchpoints. Packaging persists long after a website fix, so treat it as a separate remediation stream with print deadlines and stock-use planning. Inventory front-of-pack claims, back-of-pack paragraphs, hang tags, box interiors, inserts, and QR-linked pages. Align on-pack wording with the same evidence file used online. Where old stock carries retired claims, seek counsel advice on handling, stickers, or withdrawal rather than improvising. Plan artwork revisions with the same review worksheet and counsel verification as digital copy. Illustrative example, labeled illustrative: a fashion label schedules a packaging rerun that replaces a generic hang-tag phrase with fiber-content-specific text and a scheme reference that matches the certificate scope.

Frequently asked questions. One, does this kit make my claims lawful. No. It organizes inventory, evidence, and review. Only the exact wording, context, evidence, and applicable national rules determine the position, and counsel should verify difficult cases. Two, can I keep eco-friendly with a footnote. Standalone generic wording is the core per se risk; adding distant explanation does not cure it. Reword to specific language. Three, what about claims already live. Inventory them first, score them, remediate high-risk items promptly, and record decisions; do not wait for a campaign relaunch. Four, do influencer posts count. Yes. Brief, approve, and archive them like your own channels. Five, what if evidence is partial. Mark it partial, narrow the claim to the proven scope, or remove until evidence is complete.

More frequently asked questions. Six, can I compare against competitors. Comparisons need a stated baseline, identical scope, verifiable method, and current data; otherwise remove or reword. Seven, can I use a supplier declaration alone. Only with a verification plan, date, scope match, and renewal trigger; otherwise treat as partial. Eight, what about packaging already printed. Treat as a separate stream with counsel advice on stock handling and reprint timing. Nine, how often should I re-review. Quarterly for evergreen claims, before every campaign, and whenever evidence expires, products reformulate, or guidance changes. Ten, what records should I keep. Inventory versions, evidence exhibits, scores, worksheets, counsel notes, before-and-after captures, and publication logs. Eleven, who approves new claims. A named reviewer plus counsel verification before publication, without exception.

Further frequently asked questions. Twelve, are images really claims. Imagery contributes to overall impression and can support a misleading finding even when words are narrow; review visuals with the same care as copy. Thirteen, can a certification solve everything. Only when the scheme criteria genuinely cover the claimed attribute, verification is independent and current, and use follows scheme rules. Fourteen, what about legal-requirement claims. Presenting a mandatory feature as distinctive is separately prohibited; remove such framing. Fifteen, how do I handle translations. Approve each language version against the same evidence and scoring, not by machine-converting the headline. Sixteen, what if a marketplace adds a badge automatically. Correct the data, contact the platform, keep records, and re-check after catalog updates.

Appendix A glossary. Generic claim means a broad environmental statement without clear specification in immediate proximity. Certification scheme means a documented set of criteria with transparent governance and independent verification. Part-for-whole means presenting a component benefit as a whole-product or whole-business benefit.
