# Freelancer Scope Control - Complete Guide

## Introduction: who this kit serves and what it solves

This guide serves freelancers who earn income alone on fixed-price and hourly contracts, including writers, developers, and designers. It addresses scope creep, meaning work that expands beyond the agreed deliverables without added pay, added time, or written approval. The kit provides a plain method for defining numbered deliverables, writing an exclusions list, mapping milestones to deliverables, limiting revisions, pricing change through a change-order process, applying a 15-minute courtesy rule, and responding to extra requests with say-yes-and-quote scripts.

The kit contents include a statement of work template with exclusions, a milestone map worksheet, a change-order form, a revision counter log, a rate card template, and client pushback scripts. The workbook contains five sheets: scope register, milestone map, change-order log with billed-amount formulas, revision counter, and rate card. The templates package contains four documents: statement of work with exclusions clause, change-order form, pushback message scripts, and revision-limit clause insert.

This guide explains the problem background, the step-by-step method, daily workflows, checklists, worked illustrative examples labeled illustrative, frequently asked questions, limitations, and a disclosure section. All client situations, names of deliverables, dates, fees, and message texts in examples are illustrative. They show how to use the forms, not real client matters. Only the numbers stated in the product brief are treated as fixed product facts: the 15-minute courtesy rule, three to five workbook sheets, two to four document templates, and the price of 39 dollars. Every other number in this guide appears inside an illustrative example or as a structural step number.

How to use this guide: read it once end to end, fill the rate card with real rates, draft the next statement of work with numbered deliverables and exclusions before starting work, map each milestone to deliverables, then log requests daily and issue change orders before doing changed work. Consistent written use matters more than any single form. A form filed but never referenced during client conversation changes little. A short written record updated daily changes the entire tone of scope discussion because both sides can point to the same page.

## 1. Problem background: why scope expands on solo contracts

Scope expands for predictable reasons. First, proposals describe outcomes in broad language such as site refresh, brand update, or content support. Broad language lets the client picture one thing while the freelancer pictures another. Neither side notices until review. Second, contracts tie payment to calendar milestones rather than to defined outputs. A milestone such as week two payment arrives whether or not the deliverable behind it is defined, so unfunded work drifts between milestones. Third, revisions have no stated boundary. When a contract says revisions included without a number, each round feels free to the requester and costly to the provider. Fourth, small requests arrive informally by message and get handled verbally. Each small favor sets a precedent that the next request cites. Fifth, solo earners avoid hard conversations because the same person who delivers the work also preserves the relationship. Without prepared language, the freelancer accepts extra work to protect goodwill and absorbs the cost quietly.

Fixed-price contracts concentrate these effects because the fee is locked while the work stays open. Hourly contracts soften the fee effect but keep the other effects: vague acceptance criteria stretch review cycles, unfunded milestones delay payment for hours already worked, and unlimited revisions extend the engagement past its planned end. In both cases the visible symptom is unpaid hours. The workable cause is missing written boundaries at the start plus missing written records during delivery.

Common expansion patterns recur across trades. A writer agrees to a services page and receives successive requests for additional pages framed as quick additions. A developer agrees to a defined integration and receives requests for extra endpoints, extra formats, and extra admin screens. A designer agrees to a defined set of screens or layouts and receives requests for extra concepts, extra sizes, and extra file formats. Each request alone sounds minor. Together they equal an additional engagement delivered without a fee, a date change, or a signature. The client often acts without intent to exploit. From the client side, the request feels adjacent to the original ask. The freelancer needs a structure that treats adjacency as a pricing question rather than a favor question, and that does so in cooperative language.

Another pattern is the moving acceptance standard. Early review focuses on structure. Later review introduces new stakeholders, new preferences, and new criteria that earlier drafts could not have met. Without written acceptance criteria recorded before drafting, every round restarts the brief. Another pattern is milestone drift, where the freelancer completes draft work while the client delays content, access, or approvals, then expects the original end date to hold despite the late input. Another pattern is the perpetual polish loop, where rounds continue because no one defined what finished looks like. The method in this kit answers each pattern with a specific written instrument: acceptance criteria answer moving standards, milestone mapping with entry criteria answers drift, revision limits with a counter answer the polish loop, and change orders answer adjacency.

## 2. Method principles: the seven controls in plain terms

The method rests on seven controls that work as a set. First, numbered deliverables: every output has a number, a title, a format, and acceptance criteria. If work has no number, it is not yet in scope. Second, exclusions list: the statement of work states what is not included. Exclusions convert silence into a boundary the freelancer can cite without improvising. Third, milestone-to-deliverable mapping: each milestone names the deliverables that complete it, the entry conditions that must hold before work starts, and the exit conditions that mark it done. Payment ties to milestones whose contents are defined. Fourth, revision limits: each deliverable carries a stated number of included rounds, a definition of a round, and an overage rate. A revision controverts criteria already written, while anything outside criteria is a change. Fifth, change-order process: any addition, alteration, or new requirement uses one form that records description, fee impact, schedule impact, and signatures before work starts. Sixth, the 15-minute courtesy rule: a small request taking 15 minutes or less, which does not alter deliverables or schedule, may be handled once as a logged courtesy. Repeat, larger, or schedule-affecting requests use change orders. Seventh, say-yes-and-quote scripts: prepared messages that accept the relationship and price the change in the same breath, stating fee and date plainly.

Three habits hold the controls together. Write before working: no changed work starts before written approval. Log the same day: every request enters the scope register on the day it arrives, with minutes spent and action taken. Tie money to paper: fees, rates, and payment timing appear in the statement of work and change orders, never only in chat. These habits sound administrative. In practice they carry the commercial weight of the engagement because memory of verbal discussion diverges within days while a dated log does not.

Language discipline matters. The kit uses cooperative, specific phrasing throughout. Cooperative means the freelancer says yes to helping and states the commercial terms plainly. Specific means each message names the deliverable number, the fee, and the date. Vague firmness such as this is out of scope without more detail invites argument. Specific firmness such as that item sits outside deliverables D1 through D3, and the added fee with a revised date, invites a decision. The pushback scripts in the templates package model this phrasing so the freelancer does not compose boundary language under pressure.

## 3. Numbered deliverables: writing outputs that can be accepted

A numbered deliverable has five elements: number, title, format, acceptance criteria, and due milestone. Numbering starts at D1 and continues in order. Titles name the output in client terms. Format states the tangible form such as document, code branch, layered file, or exported set. Acceptance criteria state the test the deliverable must pass. Due milestone ties the item to the schedule map.

Write acceptance criteria as observable checks. A sound criterion names content, quantity, and quality signals that a reviewer can verify without asking what the freelancer meant. For written work, criteria name topic coverage, length band in words stated as an illustrative range, structure, and source handling. For software work, criteria name behavior, inputs, outputs, error handling, and test evidence. For design work, criteria name screens or layouts, states, sizes, formats, and asset delivery. Avoid criteria that depend on taste alone, such as clean or modern, unless paired with references the client approved in writing before drafting. A reference approved after drafting cannot fairly judge the draft.

Keep each deliverable reviewable in one sitting. When an output grows too large to review at once, split it into numbered parts with separate criteria. Large single deliverables produce large single review rounds where new feedback arrives late and contradicts early feedback. Smaller numbered parts produce focused rounds and clearer acceptance.

State dependencies inside the deliverable entry. If D2 needs client content, access, or approval of D1, write that need as an entry condition with a date. Dependencies written early convert later delays into schedule facts rather than personal friction. When the client misses an input date, the milestone map shows the effect without the freelancer arguing the point.

Hourly engagements use numbered deliverables the same way. The difference is that the fee section states the hourly rate, billing increments, and any cap, while the deliverables still define what each block of hours produces and what counts as done. Hours without deliverables drift. Deliverables without hours still need rates for overage rounds and changes, which the rate card supplies.

Review the deliverable list with the client before signature and ask for corrections in writing. The question to pose is simple: if each item arrived exactly as described, would the engagement feel complete. Gaps surface at this stage while the price can still reflect them. Gaps found after signature become change orders. Both outcomes are manageable once the list exists. Without the list, gaps become unpaid work.

## 4. Exclusions list: converting silence into a boundary

The exclusions clause states work the fee does not cover. It begins with a general sentence that anything not listed under numbered deliverables is excluded, then names the exclusions clients most often assume are included. Typical entries include additional concepts beyond the stated count, additional formats beyond the stated set, third-party costs such as stock assets and licenses, client-side responsibilities such as content and approvals, and downstream tasks such as deployment support beyond a stated window. Each engagement adds its own trade-specific exclusions. Writers exclude extra pages, extra interview rounds, and source research beyond a stated depth. Developers exclude extra endpoints, extra platforms, and data migration beyond a stated source. Designers exclude extra concepts, extra sizes, motion work, and print preparation unless listed.

Write exclusions in plain terms the client can picture. A vague exclusion such as additional work excluded helps little because the client cannot recognize the boundary when crossing it. A concrete exclusion such as two homepage concepts included, further concepts priced per change order, lets the client self-sort requests before sending them. Concrete exclusions reduce the number of pushback conversations because the statement of work already answered the common asks.

Place the exclusions clause immediately after the deliverables so the reader encounters inclusion and exclusion as one thought. Require initials or a signature on the page that carries both. During delivery, cite the clause by number and quote its short text rather than paraphrasing. Quotation keeps the conversation anchored to paper. Paraphrase invites reinterpretation.

Update exclusions between engagements. After each project, add the two or three requests that actually arrived but were not covered. Over several engagements the clause becomes a fitted record of where scope tries to expand in this practice. Keep the list readable. A clause that runs for pages signals distrust. A clause that names the frequent boundary crossings signals competence.

## 5. Milestone-to-deliverable mapping: tying payment to defined outputs

The milestone map connects schedule, outputs, and payment. Each milestone has a code, a name, linked deliverables, a due date, a payment amount where applicable, entry criteria, exit criteria, and status. Entry criteria state what must be true before work on the milestone starts, such as signed brief, received content, granted access, or approved prior deliverable. Exit criteria state what marks the milestone complete, such as delivered files plus written acceptance. Status tracks progress in plain words such as not started, in progress, awaiting client input, or done.

Build the map from deliverables rather than from calendar dates. List the deliverables first, group them into reviewable batches, then assign dates and payments to each batch. A milestone that contains no deliverable is a calendar billing event, not progress, and should be labeled as such or restructured. A milestone that contains too many deliverables becomes unreviewable and should be split. The workbook milestone map sheet supports this structure with columns for code, name, linked deliverables, dates, payments, entry and exit criteria, and status.

Entry criteria deserve emphasis because they prevent the most expensive delay pattern: the freelancer waiting on inputs while the end date stays fixed. When entry criteria include client content by a stated date and that date passes, the map shows the milestone as awaiting client input with the downstream dates shifted by the same span. The conversation becomes arithmetic rather than personal. Record the shift in the change-order log where the fee is zero but the schedule impact is stated, so the record stays complete even when no money changes hands.

Exit criteria deserve equal care. A milestone exits when its deliverables meet acceptance criteria and the client confirms in writing. Verbal approval followed by later rework is the failure mode. Require a short written acceptance per milestone, even a one-line message naming the milestone code and the word accepted. File it with the scope register. Acceptance at each milestone prevents late reopening of early work under the label of final review.

For hourly work, milestones mark review and billing points rather than fixed-fee gates. The map still names deliverables per milestone and records hours against them. The payment column then references the billing cycle rather than a fixed amount. The discipline is identical: defined outputs per period, written acceptance per period, logged hours per deliverable.

## 6. Revision limits and the revision counter: ending the polish loop

A revision round means one consolidated set of comments applied within the original acceptance criteria. Three phrases in that definition each carry weight. Consolidated means the client gathers feedback into one set per round rather than sending sequential notes that each restart the round. Within the original acceptance criteria means requests outside criteria are changes, not revisions, even when labeled as feedback. One set per round means the counter advances once per consolidated delivery of comments, not once per comment.

The statement of work states the number of included rounds per deliverable, with an illustrative count of two used throughout the kit examples. The actual number for a real engagement is a commercial choice the freelancer fills before signature. The clause also states the overage rate per additional round, drawn from the rate card, and the turnaround per round. The revision-limit clause insert template provides paste-ready text with bracket-free fields the freelancer completes, avoiding placeholder marks.

The revision counter log implements the clause. One row per deliverable records rounds included, dates of each round, extra rounds used, overage rate, and extra billed computed as extra rounds times rate. The workbook computes extra billed automatically once extra rounds and rate are entered. Review the counter with the client when included rounds are exhausted, using a script that states the fact and quotes the next round. The message pattern is short: included rounds used, next round fee, delivery date, request for confirmation. Long justification weakens the boundary. A dated count strengthens it.

Consolidation needs enforcement. When feedback arrives scattered across messages, the freelancer acknowledges receipt, asks for one consolidated set by a stated date, and notes that the round starts on receipt of the consolidated set. This single habit removes a large share of round inflation because scattered comments otherwise multiply rounds without any party intending it. State the consolidation rule in the statement of work so the request reads as procedure rather than resistance.

Distinguish taste iterations from criteria failures. When the freelancer missed written criteria, the correction belongs inside the current round without charge. When the client introduces new criteria, the work belongs in a change order even when phrased as a revision. The test is textual: compare the request to the written criteria. If the criteria already required it, revise. If not, quote. Keep the comparison visible to the client by citing criterion numbers. Visible comparison keeps the discussion factual.

## 7. Change-order process: pricing change before doing it

The change-order form records one requested change per form with identification, description, impact assessment, and approval. Identification links the change to the original statement of work reference and numbers it for the log, with illustrative numbering in kit examples. Description states the current agreed item and the requested change in plain terms plus the reason. Impact assessment states added fee, schedule impact in days, revised milestone dates, and payment terms. Approval captures signatures and status before any changed work begins.

Operate the process in five moves. First, receive the request and log it in the scope register the same day with minutes spent. Second, classify it as in-scope or out-of-scope by comparing it to deliverable criteria and exclusions. Third, answer with a say-yes-and-quote message that states fee and date. Fourth, send the change-order form and pause the affected work until signed approval returns. Fifth, record the outcome in the change-order log, where billed amount counts only when an approval date is present, and update the milestone map dates. The workbook formulas support the fourth and fifth moves by computing billed amounts from approval presence rather than from discussion.

Price changes with the rate card, not by feel. The rate card lists service codes, descriptions, units, and rates that the freelancer fills with real figures before use. Example rates in the kit are illustrative and must be replaced. When a change resembles listed services, cite the card line. When it does not, state a fixed fee for the change with the basis in one line. Consistency across changes matters more than precision in any single quote because clients accept prices that follow a visible pattern.

Handle small changes with the same form. The temptation is to waive paperwork for minor items. Minor items in sequence equal a major unbilled expansion. The form takes minutes and preserves the record. Where the 15-minute courtesy rule applies, log the courtesy in the scope register and note the rule. Where it does not, send the form. The deciding factors are repeat frequency, schedule effect, and whether deliverables or criteria change. Any schedule effect or criteria change uses the form regardless of size.

Handle declined changes plainly. When the client declines the quoted change, record the status as declined, confirm the original scope remains, and continue. Do not carry declined work forward informally. The log entry protects both sides by showing what was offered, what was declined, and what the agreed scope therefore remains.

## 8. The 15-minute courtesy rule: small kindness with a written edge

The courtesy rule states that a small request taking 15 minutes or less, which does not alter deliverables, criteria, or schedule, may be handled once as a courtesy and logged. The rule has four boundaries. Time boundary: work beyond 15 minutes does not qualify. Content boundary: anything altering deliverables or criteria does not qualify. Schedule boundary: anything moving dates does not qualify. Frequency boundary: a repeated request of the same kind does not qualify twice, because repetition proves it is a requirement rather than a courtesy.

Apply the rule visibly. When handling a courtesy, state in one line that the item is handled as a one-time courtesy under the 15-minute rule and that similar items will use change orders. Log minutes spent in the scope register. The visible statement prevents the courtesy from becoming precedent. Silent favors become expected service. Labeled favors remain favors.

Track courtesy frequency per client. When the log shows several courtesy entries in one engagement, raise the pattern at the next milestone review and propose adding the recurring item to scope by change order or adjusting the rate card. The log makes this conversation factual. Without the log, the freelancer feels drained but cannot name the drain. With the log, both sides see the pattern and can price it.

The rule also protects goodwill. A practice with no courtesy mechanism forces every minor ask into paperwork, which strains cooperative tone. A practice with an explicit, bounded courtesy shows generosity inside limits. Clients generally respect a stated limit more than an unstated reluctance because the limit reads as professional method rather than personal unwillingness.

## 9. Say-yes-and-quote scripts: cooperative language that states terms

Say-yes-and-quote means each response to an extra request contains three elements in order: acknowledgment, scope location, and priced path forward. Acknowledgment thanks the client and names the request. Scope location cites the deliverable numbers and exclusions clause. Priced path states the fee, the schedule effect, and the next step, usually the change-order form. The templates package provides five scripts in this shape covering out-of-scope requests, extra revision rounds, rush requests, unfunded milestones, and vague briefs.

Adapt scripts without softening terms. Replace the bracket-free fields with real amounts and dates, keep the fee and date in every message, and keep one request per message. Avoid apologies that invite negotiation of the boundary itself. A short factual tone serves both sides because it shortens the interval between request and decision. Long explanations extend discussion and shift focus from terms to feelings.

Send scope messages in writing and file them. Verbal scope agreements decay within days as each side remembers the friendlier version. A written message with fee and date, followed by a signed form, holds. When the client presses verbally, acknowledge warmly and restate that the written quote stands, then point to the form. Consistency across channels teaches the client that written terms are the real terms.

Prepare for the common replies. When the client says the request is small, answer with the minutes estimate and the applicable path: courtesy where it truly fits, otherwise the form. When the client says the budget is fixed, offer scope choices inside the fixed fee, such as swapping an item of equal effort or deferring an item to a later phase, each recorded by change order with zero added fee but stated scope movement. When the client says the deadline cannot move, offer the rush path with its fee or a reduced-scope path that holds the date. Choices preserve agency while keeping every path priced and dated.

## 10. Daily and weekly workflows: intake, delivery, and closing

Intake workflow for a new engagement runs in order. Qualify the request by naming the outputs the client wants and the date needed. Draft numbered deliverables with acceptance criteria and an exclusions list. Fill the rate card lines the engagement will use. Build the milestone map with entry and exit criteria. Send the statement of work and walk through deliverables, exclusions, revisions, courtesy rule, and change process on a short call. Record corrections in writing and sign. Open the scope register, change-order log, and revision counter with the engagement reference. This sequence takes care before work starts so that pressure during work does not force improvisation.

Delivery workflow runs daily. Log each incoming request the same day with description, scope classification, related deliverable number, action, and minutes. Advance revision counts as rounds arrive. Send quotes and forms before doing changed work. Update milestone statuses as entry conditions clear and exits complete. Hold a brief weekly review of the three logs with attention to repeat courtesies, pending change orders, and milestones awaiting client input. The weekly review converts scattered entries into decisions: price the repeat, chase the pending form, or shift dates with a written record.

Closing workflow runs per milestone and at engagement end. Collect written acceptance per milestone naming the milestone code. Invoice the tied amount where the contract is fixed-price, or the logged hours where hourly. Reconcile the change-order log so every approved change shows an approval date and billed amount. Reconcile the revision counter so extra rounds show billed amounts. Archive the statement of work, logs, forms, acceptances, and invoices as one set. Carry lessons into the exclusions list and rate card for the next engagement. A filed set also shortens any later payment discussion because the paper already answers what was agreed, what changed, and what was approved.

Hourly variations keep the same intake and logging shape. The intake states rate, increments, cap if any, and billing cycle. Daily logging adds hours per deliverable alongside request entries. Weekly review checks hours against the cap trajectory and deliverable progress together, so pacing problems surface while correction is still possible. Closing reconciles logged hours to invoices with deliverable acceptance as the quality anchor.

## 11. Checklists: pre-signature, mid-project, and payment

Pre-signature checklist: deliverables numbered with format and criteria, exclusions written concretely, milestones mapped with entry and exit criteria, revision count and overage rate filled, courtesy rule stated, change process stated, rate card completed for services in play, payment timing tied to milestones, acceptance method stated, signatures and dates present.

Mid-project checklist: scope register current to today, each request classified, quotes sent for out-of-scope items, forms signed before changed work, revision counter current, consolidation rule enforced, milestones aged correctly with awaiting-input flags where true, repeat courtesies flagged, pending forms chased once in writing.

Payment checklist: milestone exits documented in writing, tied amounts invoiced, approved change amounts invoiced, extra revision amounts invoiced, declined changes left out of invoices but present in the log, receipts and acceptances filed with the engagement set.

Use the checklists as written gates rather than retrospective summaries. A pre-signature gate prevents vague starts. A mid-project gate prevents silent drift. A payment gate prevents invoicing gaps. Each gate takes minutes and repays its time at the next difficult conversation.

## 12. Worked illustrative examples labeled illustrative

Example one, illustrative: fixed-price services page. A writer agrees to deliverables D1 through D3 for a fixed fee, with two included revision rounds per deliverable and an exclusions list naming extra pages as excluded. Mid-project the client requests an additional page framed as a quick addition. The freelancer logs the request in the scope register as out-of-scope against no existing deliverable number, answers with the out-of-scope script stating the added fee and a revised date for the affected milestone, and sends a change-order form numbered in illustrative form. The client approves in writing. The freelancer records the approval date, updates the milestone map, and delivers the added page on the revised date. The billed amount appears in the change-order log because an approval date is present. Had the client declined, the log would show declined status, the original scope would stand, and delivery would continue without the added page. The commercial effect is contained either way because the decision precedes the work.

Example two, illustrative: hourly integration with revision pressure. A developer on an hourly engagement defines D1 as one integration behavior with written acceptance criteria and two included review rounds for the acceptance demonstration. Review round one arrives as scattered messages across three days. The freelancer acknowledges, requests one consolidated set by a stated date, and notes the round starts on receipt of the consolidated set. The consolidated set arrives, the freelancer applies corrections inside criteria, and the counter advances one round. Round two arrives with new requirements outside criteria. The freelancer classifies the new items as out-of-scope, quotes them against the rate card with a fixed fee and schedule note, and sends a change-order form. The client approves part of the change and defers the rest. The approved part proceeds on the quoted terms. The deferred part is logged for a later phase. Hours for both the revision work and the change work are logged against their respective references so the invoice separates base scope from change scope.

Example three, illustrative: design engagement with courtesy pattern. A designer agrees to a defined set of layouts with two included rounds and a 15-minute courtesy rule. During delivery the client sends three small text and color adjustments, each taking under 15 minutes and touching nothing structural. The designer handles the first as a logged courtesy with a one-line notice that similar items will use change orders. The second and third, being repeats of the same kind, use the extra-revision script path with a quoted fee even though each is small, because the frequency boundary now applies. The log shows one courtesy and two priced items. At milestone review the designer proposes adding a small-adjustment allowance to any follow-on phase by change order. The client accepts because the log demonstrates the pattern without argument.

## 13. Frequently asked questions

Question: does this method suit hourly contracts. Answer: yes. Numbered deliverables, exclusions, revision counting, and change orders operate identically. Only the fee section changes to rate, increments, cap if any, and billing cycle. Log hours against deliverable numbers so invoices and acceptance stay linked.

Question: what if the client refuses to sign change orders but asks for the work. Answer: log the request, restate the quote in writing, and do not start the changed work before approval. Offer scope choices inside the current fee where possible, such as a swap of equal effort recorded with zero added fee. Continue the agreed scope meanwhile. Starting changed work without approval converts the form into decoration.

Question: how many revision rounds should be included. Answer: the kit examples use two as an illustrative count. The real number is a commercial decision per engagement and per trade. Whatever number is chosen, write it in the statement of work with the overage rate and enforce consolidation so rounds stay countable.

Question: what if feedback arrives from several stakeholders at once. Answer: require one consolidated set through a single contact by a stated date. Record the contact name in the statement of work. Rounds start on receipt of the consolidated set. This term belongs in paper before it is needed in conversation.

Question: how should rush requests be handled. Answer: quote a rush fee from the rate card and a revised schedule that names what pauses, then record both by change order. Rush without a written schedule change compresses all other work silently. Written rescheduling keeps the compression visible and priced.

Question: what if the client supplies late inputs. Answer: mark the milestone awaiting client input on the date the entry condition failed, shift downstream dates by the same span, and record a zero-fee schedule change so the record shows cause and effect. Resume the same day inputs arrive and confirm resumption in writing.

Question: can the courtesy rule be skipped. Answer: yes where the practice prefers all changes on forms. The rule exists to preserve goodwill for genuinely minor one-time items. Skipping it entirely is coherent. Applying it without its frequency boundary is not, because silent repetition becomes precedent.

Question: does this kit replace legal review. Answer: no. The templates are general operational starting points with plain language. Jurisdiction rules, enforceability, and dispute handling require qualified counsel. Have each statement of work reviewed before reliance in a high-value or high-risk engagement.

## 14. Limitations

This kit provides general templates, worksheets, and method guidance. It is not legal advice and does not create an attorney-client relationship. Template language may not satisfy the rules of any particular jurisdiction and may need adaptation before use. The kit cannot compel a client to sign, approve, pay, or follow a process. It cannot collect payment or resolve disputes. It cannot convert hostile negotiations into cooperative ones, though clear paper generally shortens them. Effectiveness depends on consistent use: forms completed before signature, logs updated daily, quotes sent before changed work, and acceptance collected in writing. An unused kit changes nothing. A partially used kit helps partially. No income, retention, or payment outcome is promised. Pricing choices, including the illustrative rates and counts in kit examples, must be replaced with real figures suited to the trade, experience, and market before any reliance.

Hourly and fixed-price engagements each carry limits the kit does not remove. Hourly work still requires trust in reported time and still needs client-side review discipline. Fixed-price work still requires accurate estimation and still carries the risk that defined work takes longer than planned. The kit bounds what counts as the work. It does not bound how long defined work takes. Estimation judgment remains the freelancer's responsibility.
