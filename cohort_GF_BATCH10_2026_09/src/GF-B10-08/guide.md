# DTC Returns Abuse Playbook: Tiered Policy Design with Fair, Graduated Responses

This guide helps small direct-to-consumer sellers in apparel, beauty, and similar categories reduce abusive returns without punishing sound customers. It provides a tiered policy design, structured return reasons, photo requirements on damage claims, account-level risk scoring, graduated responses that warn before restricting, and a monthly tuning routine.

Scope: this kit covers policy wording structure, case logging with scoring formulas, inspection discipline, denial and appeal workflow with reason letters, and a monthly tuning sheet. It does not provide legal advice and does not promise loss reduction. Policy language must be reviewed against applicable consumer protection rules and payment processor terms for your situation.

How to use: read Sections 1 through 6 in order, adopt the return reason list, set up the case log, define your tiers, train inspection, launch the photo requirement on damage claims, use graduated responses, and tune monthly.

A note on numbers: any case counts, scores, thresholds, dollar amounts, or day counts in worked examples are illustrative only. They show how to enter cases, compute scores, and apply graduated steps. They are not data about real stores and are not statistics about returns in general. The only fixed product facts are the kit contents described in the listing file.


## 1. Who this kit serves

This kit serves founders, operations leads, and customer service leads at small DTC sellers, with attention to apparel and beauty where fit, shade, and condition disputes are frequent. Readers typically handle returns themselves or with a small team, write policy text in plain language, and want a consistent way to separate one-time fit issues from repeated patterns that suggest wardrobing, empty-box claims, or serial returning.

Who this kit is not for: large retailers with dedicated fraud teams and custom tooling, sellers who want automated chargeback software, sellers who want legal review of policy enforceability in every jurisdiction, and sellers outside consumer goods whose return reasons differ sharply. This kit is a structured manual system with workbook formulas, not an installed software product.

What changes when you adopt it: every return becomes a logged case with a structured reason, condition evidence where required, an account-level pattern view, a risk score computed by formula from visible factors, and a graduated response tied to the score and history. Sound customers with occasional returns stay in a standard path. Repeated high-signal patterns move through warn, then restrict, with appeal rights preserved.


## 2. Problem background

### 2.1 A meaningful share of returns can be abusive, but blunt rules backfire

Sellers in fit-sensitive categories see several abusive patterns: wardrobing (item worn or used then returned as new), empty-box or wrong-item returns, damage claims for items that arrived intact, and serial returning where most orders come back. These patterns cost shipping both ways, inspection time, lost inventory value, and service load.

The common blunt responses each carry costs. Tightening windows for everyone frustrates sound customers who need exchanges. Adding restocking fees broadly suppresses legitimate sales. Denying claims without written reasons and appeal invites disputes and processor scrutiny. Ignoring patterns entirely lets repeated abuse continue.

The method here takes a middle path: keep a welcoming standard policy for ordinary returns, add structure that makes abuse visible (reasons, photos, inspection, account history), score risk at the account level, and respond in graduated steps with written reasons and appeal. Monthly tuning keeps thresholds aligned with observed cases.

### 2.2 Why structure matters more than strictness

Strictness without structure produces arguments. Structure without excessive strictness produces records. When every case carries the same fields (order reference, item, reason from a fixed list, condition on arrival, photo status, inspection result, account history), patterns emerge that anecdotes hide. A single worn-item return may reflect a one-time misunderstanding. Three similar cases on one account with matching reason shifts across orders tells a different story. The case log plus the customer pattern summary make that story readable without relying on memory.

### 2.3 Definitions used in this kit

Standard return: a case within policy terms with condition consistent with the stated reason. High-signal case: a case with one or more abuse indicators (for example, wear or use signs on a new-claim return, missing parts, mismatched item, damage claim without requested photos, or rapid repeat of high-cost reasons on one account). Risk score: a formula-computed number from visible case and account factors that ranks cases for review priority. It is a triage aid, not a finding. Graduated response: a sequence that starts with education and warning and only then moves to limits, with each step documented and appealable. Abuse finding: a conclusion reached after inspection evidence plus account pattern plus written reason review, never from a score alone.

### 2.4 Small-team reality

Small teams cannot inspect every parcel for an hour or write essays per case. The kit is designed for minutes per case: select a reason from the list, request photos where required, log condition at intake, score by formula, and follow the tiered path. Inspection checklists are short and visual. Letters use fill-in structure. Tuning happens once per month, not daily.


## 3. Tiered policy design

### 3.1 Three tiers with plain language

Tier 1, Standard: the default path for most customers. Clear window, clear condition standard, exchanges and refunds as stated, no extra steps beyond the reason selection and standard inspection. Language stays welcoming and specific.

Tier 2, Review: triggered by defined signals (for example, repeated high-cost reasons in a short span, damage claims without photos after request, or condition mismatches at inspection). Adds steps: photo requirement, inspection hold, written summary of pattern, and a warning letter that states facts, cites policy lines, and explains what will happen if the pattern continues. The customer keeps full appeal rights.

Tier 3, Restricted: applied only after documented prior warning plus continued pattern plus inspection evidence, with written reasons and appeal. Limits are narrow and time-bound (for example, exchanges only for a defined period, or a hold on instant refunds pending inspection). Blanket lifetime bans without review are outside this kit. Every restriction carries an end date and a review path.

### 3.2 Writing policy text that is firm and fair

State the window, condition standard, what is excluded and why, how to start a return, what photos are required for damage claims, how long inspection takes, how refunds are issued, and how appeals work. Cite each limit in plain terms. Avoid humor, sarcasm, or accusatory language. A customer reading the policy should understand what happens, when, and how to appeal.

Have policy text reviewed for consistency with applicable consumer protection rules and with payment processor and marketplace terms where relevant. This kit provides structure and sample wording shape. It does not provide legal clearance.

### 3.3 Structured return reasons

Use a fixed reason list so cases can be compared. Suggested list: size or fit, shade or color mismatch, changed mind, gift issue, defective on arrival, damaged in transit, wrong item sent, used or worn concern flagged at inspection, missing parts, quality concern with detail. Require staff to select one primary reason plus an optional secondary flag. Free-text detail supplements the selection but never replaces it. Monthly tuning reviews reason distribution for drift.

### 3.4 Photo requirement on damage claims

Require photos for damage or defect claims before approval: outer packaging, item condition, labels or seals where relevant, and any visible damage close-up. Provide a simple notice that states what to send, where to send it, and the timeline. Log photo status per case (requested, received, waived with reason, or not required for the reason). Missing photos after request is a review signal, not an automatic denial. Pair it with inspection and history before deciding.

### 3.5 Account-level view

Score and respond at the account level, not only per case. A single odd case on a long-clean account is handled differently from the same case type as the fourth similar case in a short span. The customer pattern summary aggregates cases per account: count, reason mix, photo compliance, inspection outcomes, refund versus exchange mix, and score trend. Graduated steps reference this view, not a single case in isolation.


## 4. Risk scoring method

### 4.1 What the score includes

The workbook computes a case score plus an account pattern score from visible factors. Case factors include reason class, condition mismatch at inspection, photo status on damage claims, and value tier. Account factors include case frequency in a rolling window, repeat of the same high-cost reason, prior warnings, and prior inspection mismatch rate. Each factor contributes points per a disclosed weight table. The total ranks review priority.

Weights are starting points, not truths. Monthly tuning adjusts them based on reviewed outcomes. Document every change with date and reason in the tuning sheet.

### 4.2 What the score does not do

The score does not find abuse by itself. It prioritizes human review. No denial or restriction is issued on a score alone. Every consequential step requires inspection evidence, account history, a written reason citing policy lines and facts, and an appeal path. This separation protects sound customers who happen to score high on a single odd case.

### 4.3 Threshold discipline

Define two thresholds: review threshold (human review required before approval) and restrict-consideration threshold (case may be considered for graduated restriction only if prior warning exists plus evidence plus history). Thresholds are numbers you set and tune. Start conservatively so ordinary fit exchanges do not trigger review. Tighten only when tuning data shows repeated high-signal cases passing through unchecked. Log threshold changes.

### 4.4 Worked scoring logic in plain terms

Each case row looks up the account history, counts prior cases in the window, checks for repeat reasons, adds case-factor points and account-factor points, and produces a total. Conditional formulas flag whether the total crosses review or restrict-consideration levels. The pattern summary aggregates per account with counts and rates. The tuning dashboard charts reason mix, score distribution, and outcome mix from logged data. All formulas are visible in workbook.json and can be reimplemented in any spreadsheet tool.

## 5. Graduated responses: warn before restrict

Step 1, Standard handling: approve, exchange, or refund per policy with a brief written confirmation. Log the case and score. No extra friction.

Step 2, Education: when a soft signal appears (for example, second similar high-cost reason in a short span), include care or fit guidance with the resolution. Log the note. No warning yet.

Step 3, Formal warning: when review threshold is crossed with supporting evidence, send the denial-with-reason or warning letter variant that states dates, orders, reasons, inspection findings, policy lines, and what change is expected. Preserve appeal. Log the warning on the account.

Step 4, Limited restriction: only after a documented warning plus continued pattern plus fresh inspection evidence, apply a narrow time-bound limit (for example, exchanges only, or refunds after inspection only). State the scope, duration, review date, and appeal path in writing. Log start and end dates.

Step 5, Review and release: at the end date, review the account. If the pattern stopped, release the limit and note it. If it continued with fresh evidence, extend narrowly with fresh written reasons and fresh appeal. Never extend silently.

Denials follow the same discipline: written reason citing facts and policy lines, evidence summary, appeal instructions, and logging. The appeal review form structures second-look review by a different reviewer where team size allows, or by checklist self-review for solo founders with a cooling interval.


## 6. Inspection discipline

### 6.1 Intake logging

At parcel opening, log condition before cleaning or repackaging: seals, tags, wear signs, odor, stains, missing parts, mismatch to order, and packaging state. Record against the case ID. Attach intake photos for high-signal cases. Inspection happens before the decision, not after.

### 6.2 Inspection checklist use

The inspection log provides one row per item with condition fields and a pass, flag, or fail result plus notes. Flag means needs second look or account-history check before deciding. Fail means condition contradicts the stated reason and must be documented with specifics (what was observed, where, with photo reference). Vague notes such as item looks off are not sufficient. Describe observable facts.

### 6.3 Damage-claim photo handling

When photos are requested, log request date and channel. On receipt, compare photos to intake condition. Note consistency or inconsistency factually. When photos are missing after request plus reminder, note the gap and weigh it alongside inspection and history. Do not treat a missing photo as sole grounds for denial. Treat it as one signal among several.

### 6.4 Chain of custody basics

Keep intake, inspection, and decision notes linked by case ID. Store photos with case ID in filenames. Limit handling between intake and inspection. For solo operators, the same person can inspect and decide only through the appeal-aware checklist with a pause between inspection and decision. Where a second person is available, separate inspection from decision for high-signal cases.

## 7. Denial and appeal workflow

### 7.1 Denial-with-reason letter

Every denial or restriction uses the letter template: case and account identifiers, timeline of relevant cases with dates and reasons, inspection findings with photo references, policy lines applied, the specific decision and its scope and duration, how to appeal with deadline and channel, and who reviews appeals. Tone stays factual and neutral. No accusations about intent. Findings describe observable facts and pattern, not motives.

### 7.2 Appeal review form

Appeals are logged on receipt with date and channel. Review checks: were all cases and photos considered, was inspection documented with specifics, does the pattern hold when re-counted, was policy applied as written, was the customer statement weighed. Outcomes: uphold, modify, or reverse, each with written reasons. Log the outcome and notify the customer in plain terms. Track appeal outcomes in the tuning sheet to detect over-denial.

### 7.3 Records and timelines

Acknowledge appeals promptly, decide within the stated timeline in your policy, and communicate the result with reasons. Keep denial, appeal, and review documents linked by case and account ID. If volume spikes, triage by score but keep the same steps. Skipping steps under pressure creates the errors this system exists to prevent.


## 8. Monthly tuning routine

Once per month, work through the tuning sheet in order:

1. Export or freeze the month case rows. Confirm every case has a reason, photo status where required, inspection result, score, and outcome.
2. Review reason distribution. Flag drift such as a spike in one reason or free-text notes clustering outside the list. Update guidance or the list only with a dated note.
3. Review score distribution versus outcomes. Count cases above review threshold, warnings sent, restrictions applied, appeals filed, and appeal reversals. A high reversal rate signals thresholds or review quality need attention, not that review should be skipped.
4. Review account-level repeats. List accounts with multiple high-signal cases and check whether graduated steps were followed in order. Correct skipped warnings before tightening rules.
5. Decide tuning changes: weight adjustments, threshold moves, wording clarifications, or photo-requirement scope changes. Record each change with date, prior value, new value, and reason. Change one element at a time so effects can be read next month.
6. File the tuning summary with month, data range, decisions, and next review date.

Tuning is the control that keeps the system fair over time. Without it, thresholds drift from reality and sound customers feel the friction first.

## 9. Checklists

### Case intake checklist

- Case ID created and linked to order reference.
- Primary reason selected from fixed list; detail noted.
- Photo status logged (required for damage claims).
- Intake condition logged before repackaging.
- Account history pulled and linked.
- Score computed by formula; threshold flags noted.

### Inspection checklist

- Seals and tags checked and logged.
- Wear, use, stain, odor signs logged factually.
- Parts count and item match verified.
- Packaging state logged.
- Photos attached with case ID filenames for flagged cases.
- Result recorded as pass, flag, or fail with specifics.

### Review-before-decision checklist

- Score and threshold flag confirmed.
- Inspection specifics and photo references attached.
- Account pattern reviewed (frequency, repeat reasons, prior warnings).
- Policy lines identified by section.
- Decision type selected (approve, warn, deny, restrict) with scope and duration where applicable.
- Letter fields completed with facts and appeal path.
- Log updated with outcome and reviewer.

### Appeal checklist

- Appeal logged with date and channel.
- Full file re-read including customer statement.
- Inspection and pattern re-verified.
- Outcome (uphold, modify, reverse) with written reasons.
- Customer notified; tuning sheet updated.

### Monthly tuning checklist

- Month data frozen with complete fields.
- Reason mix and drift reviewed.
- Score versus outcome counts reviewed.
- Repeat-account handling audited for graduated order.
- Changes recorded with date and reason.
- Summary filed with next review date.

## 11. Frequently asked questions

Q: Will this system punish sound customers?
A: It is designed to avoid that by keeping a welcoming standard path, scoring for triage only, requiring evidence plus history plus written reasons before consequential steps, warning before restricting, and preserving appeal. Monthly tuning watches reversal rates for signs of overreach.

Q: What counts as abuse under this kit?
A: Observable patterns such as worn or used items returned as new, mismatched items, missing parts, damage claims inconsistent with photos and intake, and rapid repeats of high-cost reasons on one account. Findings rest on inspection facts plus pattern plus review, never on a score alone and never on suspicion about motives.

Q: Do we need photos for every return?
A: No. Photos are required for damage or defect claims and for flagged inspections. Standard size exchanges with consistent condition do not need extra steps. The notice template states what to send and where.

Q: Can a score alone deny a return?
A: No. Scores prioritize review. Denials and restrictions require inspection evidence, account history, written reasons citing policy lines, and appeal.

Q: What if the team is only one person?
A: Use the same steps with a cooling interval between inspection and decision, and use the appeal checklist for second-look review. Log reviewer identity and times so the pause is visible.

Q: How do we handle gifts?
A: Log the ordering account and the return requester separately where your system allows, and note gift context in the case. Pattern review stays at the appropriate account level per your records. Keep language neutral and focus on item condition and reason.

Q: What about exchanges versus refunds?
A: Track both in the log. Time-bound limits may favor exchanges pending inspection where policy allows. State the mix clearly in letters so the customer understands options. Review processor and policy terms for refund timing.

Q: How long should restrictions last?
A: Narrow and time-bound, with a stated review date. Release when the pattern stops. Extend only with fresh evidence and fresh written reasons plus fresh appeal. Avoid open-ended limits.

Q: What if a customer appeals?
A: Log the appeal, re-verify inspection and pattern including the customer statement, decide to uphold, modify, or reverse with written reasons, notify plainly, and record the outcome for tuning.

Q: Do we publish thresholds or weights?
A: Publish the policy path (standard, review, restricted), photo requirements, and appeal rights. Keep internal weights and thresholds as operational triage settings. Letters cite facts and policy lines, not internal scores.

Q: How do we start without history?
A: Start with the default weights and conservative thresholds, handle every case through the log from day one, and let the first monthly tuning set your baseline from real logged cases. Do not backfill history from memory.

Q: Does this kit promise loss reduction?
A: No. It provides structure, scoring, graduated steps, and tuning that support consistent handling. Results depend on execution, volume, and category. No outcome is promised.

Q: What about consumer protection and processor rules?
A: Policy text must fit applicable consumer protection rules and payment processor or marketplace terms. Have wording reviewed for your situation. This kit provides structure, not legal clearance.

Q: How do we train staff quickly?
A: Teach the reason list, photo requirement, intake logging, and the review-before-decision checklist. Shadow three cases per person before solo handling. Audit early cases for checklist completeness.

Case ID: unique row identifier. Order reference: traceable order identifier from your system. Account ID: customer account grouping key. Date received: intake date. Item and value tier: what came back and its tier. Reason primary: one selection from the fixed list. Reason detail: short factual note. Photo status: requested, received, waived with reason, or not required. Inspection result: pass, flag, or fail with specifics. Case points: formula from case factors. Account points: formula from history factors. Total score: sum with threshold flags. Outcome: approve, warn, deny, or restrict with scope. Reviewer and dates: who decided and when. Appeal status: none, pending, upheld, modified, reversed.

## Appendix B: How scoring formulas work

Case-factor formulas assign points per reason class, condition mismatch, photo gap on damage claims, and value tier using lookup tables. Account-factor formulas count prior cases in the rolling window, repeat-reason counts, prior warnings, and mismatch rate from the pattern summary. Totals sum both groups. Threshold flags use conditional comparisons to mark review-required and restrict-consideration rows. The pattern summary aggregates per account with counts, rates, and trend. The tuning dashboard summarizes reason mix, score bands, and outcome mix from logged rows. All logic is visible and adjustable with dated tuning notes.
