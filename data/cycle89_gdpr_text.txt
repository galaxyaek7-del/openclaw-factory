GDPR Data-Subject Request Handling Kit for SMBs
This kit gives a small business a complete, repeatable method for handling data-subject requests
inside the one-month response clock. You log every request with automatic deadline math, verify
identity proportionately, clarify over-broad scope into a searchable plan, search every system with a
reviewed checklist, justify each redaction on record, and assemble response packs or erasure
decisions that reconstruct cleanly when queried. Within 10 days you hold a live register, mapped data
locations, four ready templates, and a documented search-to-response method.
1. Executive Guide
Data-subject requests are the GDPR's most operationally demanding feature for small firms. An access
request obliges the business to find personal data across every system, review it for third-party content,
and deliver it within one month of receipt, with a documented two-month extension available only for
complex cases (OBSERVED: EDPB SME guide, accessed 2026-09-25). An erasure request adds a
deletion-and-retention decision with lasting consequences. Most small businesses handle these events
ad hoc: the request sits in a shared inbox, nobody owns the clock, the search covers the CRM and
stops, redactions happen by instinct, and the reasoning lives in someone's head. Each shortcut
compounds the next until the deadline arrives with an indefensible file.

month (OBSERVED: gdprdsar.com, accessed 2026-09-25). Against those anchors, a straightforward

Where small-firm searches actually fail deserves a closer look, because the CRM-only search is the
signature failure of ad hoc handling. Personal data in a small business lives in the CRM, the email
marketing list, support tickets, shared drives, accounting exports, chat histories, phone backups, and
paper files. The search matrix in section 3 exists because requesters remember the forgotten stores:
the marketing list nobody connected to the request, the archived tickets excluded from the export, the
spreadsheet a departed staffer kept locally. The mapping exercise on day 2 of the rollout enumerates
every store before any live search, and the checklist's unreviewed-system count must reach zero
before a pack ships. Coverage proved row by row is the entire credibility of an access response;
everything else is presentation.
A further point on proportionality. Small firms sometimes over-respond from anxiety: demanding
excessive identity proof, copying counsel on routine replies, or treating every request as hostile. The
kit's posture is deliberately measured. Identity evidence must be proportionate to the situation; the
template says so explicitly and the decision tree routes weak-ID cases to a single proportionate
request, not an interrogation. Clarification questions are capped at five and framed cooperatively.
Redactions protect third parties and exempt material only, logged per passage. Erasure retains what
the business must keep, with grounds and review dates, rather than deleting defensively and
destroying records the business needs. Restraint documented is as much a part of defensibility as
thoroughness documented.
The extension discipline deserves emphasis because extensions are the most abused feature of
request handling. The EDPB rule permits two further months for complex cases with reasons given to
the requester (OBSERVED: EDPB SME guide, accessed 2026-09-25). The deadline calculator tracks
standard and extended dates side by side, counts extensions in use, and requires a recorded reason
plus requester notification for each one. The worksheet in section 7 scores extension justification so the
team learns what genuinely qualifies: multi-system searches with archive involvement, high-volume
records, or parallel complex requests. A business that extends every request extends none credibly;
the counter on the sheet keeps the exception exceptional. Where in doubt, the decision tree routes to
counsel rather than to a self-granted extension.
Parallel requests deserve their own planning because they arrive in clusters, not evenly. A breach
notification, a press mention, or a departing customer's social post can generate several requests in
one week, each with its own clock. The register handles this structurally: every row carries its own
deadline and days-left flag, so triage by proximity is mechanical rather than debated. The practical
rules are three. First, never borrow time from an early-deadline request to advance a later one; work in
deadline order, always. Second, add the second searcher early: the data map names alternates per
store precisely so parallel searching starts in hours, not days. Third, brief counsel at the cluster's start
rather than per request, with the register attached; one briefing covering five rows costs far less than
five separate engagements. The extension worksheet then polices honestly which rows genuinely
qualify as complex, because a cluster feels complex without each member being so. Volume pressure
is real, but the clock does not bend for it; staffing and sequencing are the only honest answers.
The complaint path also repays understanding in advance. A requester who considers the response
late, partial, or wrongly redacted may complain to the supervisory authority, and that complaint will ask
for the file: the log row, the searches, the redaction grounds, the delivery proof. A business holding this
kit's records answers from paper in an afternoon. A business without them reconstructs from memory
under regulatory scrutiny, which is the most expensive possible context for improvisation. The QA

2. Quick Start
Complete this 45-minute first session before touching anything else, preparation included. It triages live
requests first, then maps data locations. Definition of done: every live request is logged with its
deadline computed, every system holding personal data is listed, and the next action per request has
an owner and a date.
Step 1 - Triage live requests (15 minutes). Open the inbox and any other channel where requests
arrive. Every message that is or might be a data-subject request becomes a register row immediately:
reference, requester, type, receipt date, identity status. Compute the deadline by formula. If identity is
unverified, queue the verification reply for same-day send; the clock runs from receipt regardless, so ID
chasing starts now, not later. If any request is within 7 days of deadline, it jumps the queue: all
remaining Quick Start time goes to it, and the owner is told today.
Step 2 - Map data locations (20 minutes). List every place the business holds personal data: CRM,
ticketing, marketing list, ecommerce orders, accounting, shared drives, chat tools, phone backups,
paper files, archives. For each, record who can search it and roughly how (export function, admin
search, manual review). This map becomes the search checklist's row set. Mark any store nobody can
currently search (a departed staffer's local files, an archived system without access) as GAP with an
owner and a recovery date; gaps found now are fixable, gaps found mid-request are emergencies.
Step 3 - Set the weekly rhythm (10 minutes). Assign the register owner (usually the admin or ops
staffer, with a named stand-in for absences), set a weekly 20-minute register review (deadlines, chase
dates, extension flags), and file the four templates where the handler reaches them. Record the
counsel contact for complex-case escalation. That rhythm is the definition of done: logged requests,
mapped stores, owned review.
Common first-session mistakes: debating whether a vague message counts as a request instead of
logging it (log first, classify second; over-logging costs nothing, while an unlogged message carries the
full deadline risk silently); mapping only the CRM and declaring the map complete; and treating the
deadline as starting when convenient rather than from receipt. The register's EDATE formula settles
the third mistake mechanically.
Two edge cases come up regularly. Requests arriving by phone or in person: log them with the receipt
date and a written note of what was asked, then confirm by email or letter restating the understanding;
verbal requests carry the same clock. Multiple requests from the same person: one row each, linked by
note, because deadlines and types may differ; batching them into a single row is how one deadline
gets missed. Requests from agents or family members writing on someone's behalf: require authority
evidence (written authorization or a legal basis such as parental responsibility) before disclosing
anything, and correspond with the agent only once authority is recorded; the verification reply template
adapts to this case by substituting the authority request for the ID request, with the same chase
discipline.

3. Main Toolkit
The core method runs in five stages: Intake, Verify, Clarify, Search and Review, Decide and Respond.
Each stage below is a subsection with inputs, procedure, and exit criteria.
3.1 Intake - log everything immediately
Inputs: the request message, the Request_Register sheet. Procedure: create the row within one
business day of receipt: reference (DSR-YYYY-NNN), requester, type (access, erasure, rectification,
restriction, portability, objection, or other with a note), receipt date, identity status. The deadline formula
computes the one-month date automatically; days-left flags urgency. Acknowledge receipt promptly in
plain terms without prejudging the outcome, confirming the reference number the requester should
quote in follow-ups. File the original message with the row, preserving headers where available for
authenticity. Exit criteria: row complete, deadline visible, acknowledgment sent, next action owned.
3.2 Verify - confirm identity proportionately
Inputs: the identity-verification reply template, whatever the requester has already provided. Procedure:
where identity is reasonably established (request from the account email on file, matching details),
record the basis and proceed; no reply needed. Where it is not, send the verification reply the same
day: acknowledge, state exactly what is needed, keep it proportionate (one or two items, with obscuring
guidance for documents), give the channel, and set a chase date. Never demand more than the
situation calls for; excessive demands are themselves complainable. Record the outcome: ID received
(proceed), partial (one focused follow-up), or silent past the chase date (second chase, then counsel on
whether to close or hold). Exit criteria: identity recorded with basis, or chase cycle documented with
dates.
3.3 Clarify - narrow broad scope actionably
Inputs: the scope-clarification letter template, the data-location map. Procedure: where the request is
actionable as written, skip this stage and search. Where it is too broad to search safely ("everything
you hold"), send the clarification letter within a few days: quote the original scope, ask three to five
specific questions (date range, systems, formats), and state that answers will direct the search. Record
answers as the search scope; non-answers after the reply-by date do not stop the clock, so proceed on
a reasonable interpretation recorded in writing and invite correction. Exit criteria: search scope
recorded (from answers or documented interpretation), systems to search listed.
3.4 Search and review - prove coverage
Inputs: the Search_Checklist matrix, the data-location map, the Redaction_Log. Procedure: one row
per system per request. Search each store, record hits, mark reviewed only after a human has actually
reviewed the hits (an export nobody opened is not reviewed). Log every redaction as it is made:
document, field, whose data is protected, and the ground. Assemble the access pack behind the cover
sheet: numbered enclosures, search coverage with dates, redaction references, anything withheld with
reasons, and a query route. The unreviewed-system count must read zero before the pack ships. Exit
criteria: every system row reviewed, every redaction logged, cover sheet complete, pack
reviewer-signed.

Search technique deserves practical detail. Start with the highest-yield stores (CRM, tickets, marketing
list) to establish the bulk early, then work outward to drives, chats, and archives. For email and chat
histories, search by the requester's known addresses and name variants, and record the query terms;
a later query about completeness is answered from those notes. For shared documents, check
comments and version histories where personal data hides. For archives and backups, follow the kit's
noted-rotation pattern: record the scope that was and was not restored, with the reasoning, rather than
silently skipping or needlessly restoring entire archives. Each technique note is filed per request so the
method improves with repetition.
Cover-sheet assembly has its own discipline worth stating. The cover sheet is written last but reviewed
first: it is the document the requester actually reads, and its inventory must match the enclosures
exactly. Number enclosures physically (file names or page ranges, not vague descriptions) so a
missing item is detectable. State the search coverage with dates per system; a requester who sees
four systems with dates trusts the response more than one who reads a bare assertion of
completeness. Reference each redaction by log ID so the reasoning is traceable without re-explaining.
And include the query route with a real timeframe, because follow-up questions are normal and a pack
without a contact reads as a closed door. The reviewer signs the cover sheet only after verifying each
of these against the underlying rows; the signature is the control, not a formality.
3.5 Decide and respond - close defensibly
Inputs: the assembled pack or the erasure-decision record, the Deadline_Calculator. Procedure for
access: send the pack through a tracked channel before the deadline, file delivery evidence, and diary
the 30-day query window. Procedure for erasure: complete the decision record (scope, found records,
deleted items with confirmation, retained items with grounds and review dates), execute deletion, notify
the requester with the record attached, and file everything. Where the extension applies, notify the
requester with reasons before the standard deadline expires and track the extended date. Exit criteria:
response sent with delivery filed, or erasure executed with confirmations and requester notice filed, all
inside the applicable deadline.
Erasure judgment calls deserve explicit guidance. Retention grounds in the worked example (tax
records, audit-trail IDs) are illustrative; the buyer confirms each ground for its own records against
applicable law and counsel where uncertain. Backup handling follows disclosure: tell the requester
backups expire on rotation rather than claiming instant deletion everywhere, because the latter is
usually untrue and the former is defensible. Third-party data mixed into the requester's records is
redacted under the log, not deleted wholesale, where the business must preserve its own records.
Each of these rules is stated in the decision record's guidance so the handler applies them consistently
across requests.
Non-access request types follow the same machinery with adjusted outputs. Rectification (correction)
requests: verify the claimed inaccuracy against source records, correct where wrong with a dated note
of the change, and decline with written reasons where the record is accurate and the requester
disagrees (recording their statement alongside is the recognized pattern). Restriction requests: flag the
records as restricted in each live system, confirm processing pauses except storage and agreed
purposes, and diary the restriction's review. Portability requests: export the provided-by-the-requester
data in a structured, commonly used format with the same cover-sheet inventory discipline, noting that
observed data and inferred data sit outside the portable set. Objection requests: record the objection,

3.6 Deadline and extension governance
Parallel to the stages, the Deadline_Calculator governs time. Standard and extended dates compute
by formula; extension rows require a reason and a requester-notification flag. Review extensions
weekly: the count of extensions in use should stay small, each with its documented complexity ground.
Where a deadline will be missed despite effort, the playbook is candid: inform the requester with
reasons and a delivery date, record the miss with its cause, fix the process gap in the review, and take
counsel where the miss carries dispute risk. A documented miss with a corrected process reads very
differently from a silent one discovered later.

Template 2 - Scope clarification letter. Purpose: convert over-broad requests into searchable scope

Template 3 - Access response pack cover sheet. Purpose: inventory and accountability for the finished
pack. Field-by-field: reference and requester; period covered; numbered enclosures; systems searched
with dates; redaction count with log references; anything withheld with reasons; query route with
timeframe. Worked example: the DSR-2026-031 pack sent 2026-10-02 with two logged redactions and
zero withholdings beyond them.
Template 4 - Erasure decision record. Purpose: the full audit trail behind deletion and retention.
Field-by-field: reference and requester; deletion scope; records found system by system; deleted items
with dates and confirmations; retained items with grounds and review dates; requester notice with date
and channel; approver with date. Worked example: the DSR-2026-035 record decided 2026-10-09 with
backup-rotation disclosure.
Review cadence for the templates: the identity and clarification templates are per-request
correspondence, filed with their request. The cover sheet ships per access pack with reviewer sign-off.
The erasure record is per erasure decision, retained with the retention review dates it sets. After each
real request, note any template friction (a missing field, an unclear instruction) and update the template;
quarterly, verify contact details embedded in each one. Retention review dates deserve their own diary
discipline: when a retained record's review date arrives, pull the erasure record, confirm the ground still
holds or delete then, and file the revisit note. A retention ground confirmed once and never revisited
decays into precisely the indefinite storage the requester originally challenged.

6. Decision Trees
Intake classification flow. Start: message received that may be a request. Is it from or about an
identifiable living individual and does it concern their personal data? If yes, log as a request row now;
classify the type second. If genuinely unclear, log it as a possible request with a 2-day classification
task owned by the register owner. Never leave an unlogged message while deciding; the log costs
seconds and the missed clock costs far more.

Verify-versus-proceed flow. Start: identity evidence in hand. Request from the account email on file
with matching details? Proceed, recording the basis. Unknown address or third party writing on
someone's behalf? Send the verification reply (or authority evidence for agents) same-day with a chase
date. Partial evidence? One focused follow-up naming exactly what is missing. Silent past two chases?
Counsel on close-or-hold; record the advice and follow it.
Extension flow. Start: standard deadline within 10 days and work incomplete. Is the case genuinely
complex (multi-system search with archive involvement, high-volume records, parallel complex
requests)? If yes, notify the requester with reasons before the standard deadline, record the extended
date, and track it. If no, reassign effort to finish on time: narrow remaining scope, add a searcher, or
brief counsel on partial response. Complexity discovered after the standard deadline has passed is
handled as a documented miss per section 3.6, not a retroactive extension.
Redaction flow (per passage). Start: does the passage contain third-party personal data or exempt
material? If no, disclose. If yes, can the third-party content be separated (redact the names, disclose
the rest)? If yes, log the redaction with its ground and disclose the remainder. If no (inextricably mixed),
log the withholding with its ground and state it on the cover sheet. Every branch ends in a log row; a
withheld passage without one does not exist defensibly.
Erasure flow. Start: erasure requested. Identify each record holding the requester's data. Must the
business keep it (tax law, contract performance, legal claims, other documented ground)? If yes, retain
with ground and review date recorded, and tell the requester. If no, delete, confirm deletion, and record
the confirmation. Backups: note rotation expiry to the requester rather than claiming instant erasure.
Mixed records: redact third-party content under the log where the business retains its own records.
Close with the decision record approved and the requester notified.
Follow-up query flow. Start: requester queries the response within the diary window. Is the query a
correction of fact (a missing system, a wrong date, an unexplained redaction)? If yes, treat it as a
restricted new task with its own 2-week internal target: investigate, supplement the pack or correct the
record, and reply with what changed and why. Is it dissatisfaction without new facts (disagreement with
a lawful retention, unhappiness with redactions)? If so, reply once with the grounds restated plainly and
the complaint route signposted; do not re-argue indefinitely. Is it a genuinely new request (new data
type, new period)? If so, open a fresh register row with its own clock rather than stretching the old one.
Log every query against the original reference so the full correspondence reconstructs as one thread.
Queries handled this way rarely escalate; queries ignored almost always do.
7. Worksheets
Worksheet A - Search completeness record (per request). Copy each system row with hits and review
status; total hits via the rollup formula; confirm the unreviewed count is zero. Record any GAP store
with its recovery plan. Interpretation: complete means every mapped store searched and reviewed with
evidence; a pack shipping over an incomplete matrix is the failure this worksheet exists to prevent.
Worksheet B - Extension justification score (per extension). Rate three factors 1-5: system spread
(stores involved), volume (records to review), and contention (parallel requests competing for the
handler). Total 3-15; 9+ supports extension with the reasoning recorded, below 9 means finish on time
by reassigning effort. Interpretation: the score disciplines the exception; extensions granted below the
line more than occasionally signal understaffing to fix rather than clocks to extend.

touching the deadline, which is why the reply goes same-day and the chase dates are diaried rather
than remembered. A verification reply sent a week late would have left eleven days for the entire
substantive job. Handler time beyond the waiting: about 2.5 hours (ESTIMATED). The file shows every
date, both chases, and the reasoning, so the compressed substantive phase reads as diligence, not
rush.
9. Implementation Workflow
Day 1 - Run the 45-minute Quick Start (section 2). Output: live requests logged, data map drafted,
review rhythm set. Day 2 - Complete the data map: confirm search access per store, resolve GAPs or
date their recovery, add the map's rows to the search checklist. Day 3 - Fill the business's details into
all four templates (contact addresses, handler names, counsel contact); test the deadline formulas with
a sample date. Day 4 - Run a sample search: pick a real (or test) subject, search two systems, log one
mock redaction, assemble a practice cover sheet; debrief friction. Day 5 - Process live requests
through intake to search; send verification or clarification letters where needed. Day 6 - Complete first
searches and reviews; log redactions; draft first packs or decision records. Day 7 - Week-1 checkpoint:
register current, searches provable, correspondence filed. Record open items with owners. Day 8 -
Handle responses due: reviewer sign-off, tracked sending, delivery filing, query diaries. Day 9 - Close
erasure decisions due: confirmations collected, requester notices sent, retention dates diaried. Day 10
- Final review: every checklist in section 5 walked once, retrospective template scheduled quarterly,
one process fix identified with an owner. Write the one-page closeout: requests handled, timeliness,
open items with owners and dates. Confirm the weekly 20-minute register review is in the calendar as a
recurring event with a named stand-in for the register owner's absences; the method survives holidays
only if the review does.
Contingency notes for the plan. If a deadline-critical request lands mid-rollout, it preempts everything:
the partial kit (register, one template, counsel number) handles it today while setup resumes after. If the
business discovers unsearchable stores (legacy archives, ex-staff devices), the GAP plan from section
2 governs: recovery dated and owned, interim disclosure notes drafted for affected requests. If request
volume spikes (a breach or press event generating parallel requests), triage by deadline proximity, add
a second searcher from the map's named alternates, and brief counsel early; the extension worksheet
polices which cases genuinely qualify. None of these events restarts the plan; each is handled inside
its day with owner and date recorded.

12. Disclaimer / Scope
This kit is an informational and organizational aid for handling data-subject requests. It is not legal
advice, and nothing in it predicts or promises any request outcome, authority view, or requester
response. EDPB and national data-protection authority guidance is authoritative; verify the duties that
apply to each request there and with licensed counsel before acting, especially for complex cases,
disputed identity, retention grounds, and extensions. Deadline computation follows the EDPB SME
guide as observed; the buyer remains responsible for correct application, including any
jurisdiction-specific detail. Retention grounds in examples are illustrative starting points, not
determinations. UNKNOWN items in this kit are left open deliberately rather than filled with guesses.
The buyer is responsible for confirming all decisions against primary sources and professional advice.
